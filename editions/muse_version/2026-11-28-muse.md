# AI Off-Consensus — The Entropy Brief

## Verification Splits, Governance Reverses, Markets Reprice

Run-date: 2026-11-28 · Coverage: 2026-11-21 through 2026-11-27

## Orientation: The Five Signals That Matter This Week

These are the week's five developments that most changed, or most tested, the mental model.

- First, verification split in two. On November 21 the Lean community announced full confidence that the machine-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis was correct, and days later a shorter version appeared, yet a dissenting preprint argued that a checked derivation is not the same as a proof in the ordinary sense, while the companion Navier-Stokes claim still withholds the numerical certificate that would let anyone else check it.[^1][^2][^3][^4] The anomaly is that formal checking and human understanding now move on different clocks: a proof can be machine-certified before any mathematician understands it, and certified before anyone agrees on what certification means.

- Second, Nvidia reversed itself in eleven days. Its board, which had said on November 17 that it had no intention of bringing back its founder, voted unanimously on November 23 to reinstate him as chief executive, after government input and pressure from customers and the White House, and the shares soared by about a third in a day before giving much of it back over the next two sessions.[^5][^6] The anomaly is that governance, national security and market price all changed their minds together, so the week's lesson is not that leadership is irrelevant but that it is now priced, pressured and reversible at a speed no board process was built for.

- Third, the market repriced the build-out instead of debating it. On November 25 the Nasdaq fell by about four per cent in intraday trading, chipmakers led the fall, volatility jumped above thirty, and Anthropic, which had closed its first day well above its offer price, had already fallen below that price on November 24, its fourth day of trading.[^7][^8][^9] The anomaly is the whipsaw: a record listing, a record quarter and a record financing all arrived within days of a sell-off that treated them as evidence of excess rather than of strength.

- Fourth, agents moved from the chat window onto the machine itself. On November 24 Anthropic let Claude artifacts be installed as desktop apps, with a tier of permission that lets an installed app run programs and change or delete files without asking each time, and Anthropic says it does not review apps before installation.[^10] The anomaly is that the sandbox question from last week's lawsuit now arrives on the user's own desktop, in a form the user installs voluntarily, so containment becomes a permission the user grants rather than a boundary the lab enforces.

- Fifth, quantum moved from laboratory curiosity to legislation and finance in the same week. Researchers reported entanglement across a connected region of 123 orbit qubits on November 25, Quantinuum and JPMorganChase ran error-corrected portfolio and equation-solving algorithms on a dozen logical qubits, and the European Commission proposed its first Quantum Act, with post-quantum cryptography requirements for public procurement.[^11][^12][^13] The anomaly is the pairing of a fragile laboratory result with a binding policy timetable: the hardware is still far from useful, yet the migration clock for cryptography is now being set by law.

## AI Technology

The week's central event in AI technology was not a new model but a dispute about what counts as a proof. The Riemann Hypothesis claim, posted on November 20 by an Anthropic mathematician working with Claude, was checked in Lean and endorsed on November 21 by the Lean community, which said it had full confidence in its correctness.[^1] A more efficient version followed on November 25, compressing the formalisation from about seventeen million lines to about three hundred thousand, which shows how much of the original bulk was machine-generated scaffolding rather than mathematical substance.[^2] Yet as of late November no complete human-readable account had been published, and early reviewers said the argument draws on a wide swath of modern number theory that few mathematicians command in full.

Extrapolating forward, this is a new trend, and it bears directly on the shift from generation to verification. If a landmark result can be certified before it is understood, then understanding becomes a separate, slower product from proof, and the firms and institutions that can translate machine proofs into human knowledge will hold the scarce capability. The risk is the opposite: a field that accepts certificates it cannot read will accumulate results it cannot build on, and the next proof will rest on foundations nobody has inspected.

The dissent arrived within days. A preprint argued that the certified derivation does not prove the hypothesis in the ordinary sense, because it derives the statement from premises including the consistency of the prover's own rules, and it noted that two runs of the check were still unfinished in the public record on November 23.[^3] That argument has not been accepted by the Lean community, and it should not be treated as a refutation, but it is exactly the kind of second-order verification question the model predicted: once checking is automated, the argument moves to whether the checker, the statement and the premises are the right ones.

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration of the verification problem rather than a reversal of the proof. The practical consequence for a software leader is to treat formal certificates as evidence about a formal statement, not as a substitute for understanding, and to budget for independent re-checking, statement auditing and human-readable reconstruction whenever a machine-assisted result matters for a decision.

The companion Navier-Stokes claim stayed in the weaker position. Alpöge and Buckmaster's paper, also dated November 20, claims finite-time blow-up with no external force, but its numerical certificate — the data that would let others rerun the check — had still not been released, according to reports in the research window.[^4] That is a deceleration in verification relative to the Riemann case, not a judgment on the mathematics, and it leaves the claim in the state last week's edition described: striking, human-readable in outline, and uncheckable in detail.

Extrapolating forward, the two claims together now form a natural experiment in verification regimes. One was checked quickly and compressed quickly; the other cannot yet be checked at all. Watch which regime other machine-assisted claims follow, because the choice will decide whether AI-for-science accelerates trust or merely accelerates announcement.

On models themselves, the week was quieter than the mathematics, and that quiet is itself informative. Google's Gemini 3, released on November 17 for background, continued to roll out across search and devices, and no frontier lab released a new flagship model in the window.[^14] The churn that did matter was at the other end of the lifecycle: model retirements. Claude Sonnet 4.5 is due to stop answering on November 30, and the original GPT-5 family is due to retire in December, with named successors in each case.[^15] That is a level that continues an expected trend — rapid turnover — but it matters for enterprise planning, because a model that was the default a few months ago can become an error message with a week's notice.

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration of the portfolio shift. A team that hard-codes a model name into a product will be forced to migrate on the vendor's timetable, while a team that routes by capability and cost can absorb a retirement as a routing change. The retirement calendar should be treated as a standing input to architecture review, not as a surprise.

In inference, last week's Cerebras result — disaggregated prefill and decode in production, reported on November 17 for background — received no independent replication in the window, and no rival published a comparable production measurement.[^16] That silence is worth recording rather than filling: a single-vendor production claim about throughput per megawatt remains a company claim until a second deployment reproduces it.

## Quantum Computing

Quantum computing produced the week's most concrete laboratory result and its most concrete policy move, and they point in opposite directions on timing. Researchers at the University of Science and Technology of China reported entanglement across a connected region of 123 orbit qubits, storing each qubit in the position of an atom rather than in its electronic state, in a paper published in Physical Review Letters and reported on November 25.[^11] The team did not demonstrate a computation using all of those qubits, and average fidelity in the tested region was about ninety-five per cent, well below the level fault tolerance requires.

Extrapolating forward, this is a new trend in encoding rather than an acceleration toward useful machines. A new way to store and entangle qubits matters because it attacks the control problem that has limited optical lattices, but a ninety-five per cent fidelity leaves a wide gap to close, and the result should be read as an architecture paper, not as a machine.

The error-correction picture improved in a more directly relevant way for finance. Quantinuum and JPMorganChase published work in Physical Review X on November 19, for background, running optimisation and linear-equation algorithms on error-corrected logical qubits, with the largest run using a dozen logical qubits built from nearly a hundred physical qubits.[^12] The protected runs beat random guessing and, on smaller circuits, performed about as well as unprotected ones, but the authors stopped short of claiming fault tolerance for the whole computation, and the portfolio problem was highly simplified.

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration, but a measured one. Error-corrected algorithms are now running end to end on real hardware, which is a genuine step, yet the step is from toy problems to slightly larger toy problems, and the authors' own caution about scaling is the right frame. For an asset manager, the signal is to keep quantum finance in research, not in production planning, while watching the break-even point where a protected run beats an unprotected one on a real book.

AI for quantum also advanced on paper. Research from Harvard, Anthropic and QuEra, reported for background, showed Claude designing quantum circuits that cut a combined gate-and-qubit resource measure by up to about forty times on one building block, with smaller gains on others, using an independent evaluator to check each design.[^17] Those are resource estimates for individual building blocks, not runs on hardware, and they do not amount to a speedup, but they show the feedback loop the model anticipated: AI reduces the resources quantum needs, which brings useful quantum closer without any hardware improvement.

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration of the hybrid loop. If AI-designed circuits keep cutting resource needs, the threshold for a useful quantum application falls even while hardware improves slowly, and the two curves compound. The watching point is whether the gains survive when whole algorithms, rather than isolated blocks, are optimised.

Policy moved faster than hardware. On November 25 the European Commission proposed its first Quantum Act, aiming to turn research into industrial capacity, with post-quantum cryptography requirements for public procurement and a target of about a hundred error-corrected logical qubits per system by the end of the decade.[^13] A companion equity fund was proposed alongside it, aimed at closing the growth-capital gap for quantum, AI, semiconductors and cloud.

Extrapolating forward, this is a new trend with direct consequences for post-quantum migration. A procurement rule that requires post-quantum cryptography turns migration from a prudent plan into a condition of selling to European public bodies, and the Commission's power to set binding migration milestones if national transitions lag means the timetable may be set by regulators rather than by cryptographers. Firms should treat the proposal as the start of a legislative process, not as law, but should price migration as a compliance cost that is now on a public clock.

On the industrial side, Nvidia and IBM's quantum-classical collaboration, announced on November 16 for background, continued without a new in-window milestone, though a decoding algorithm claimed to speed error-correction research by a very large multiple remains the most striking claim in that package and still awaits independent replication.[^18] Xanadu's announcement on November 27 that it is turning a large Toronto building into a photonic quantum factory, backed by substantial federal support, is a further sign that quantum is acquiring factories, not only laboratories.[^19]

## Enterprise AI Adoption

Enterprise adoption this week was shaped less by new deployments than by the plumbing underneath them: identity, checkout and retirement. The clearest in-window evidence came from agentic commerce. A study that re-ran its checks on November 24 found that only a small minority of large retailers publish the open standards an agent needs to browse and buy, while direct-to-consumer brands did markedly better, and the largest retailers are instead choosing which assistant may sell for them through direct deals.[^20] Amazon, the study found, turns away the shopping agents of the major assistants while running its own.

Extrapolating forward, this is a new trend, and it bears on the portfolio and the agent-driven demand subdimensions at once. The open agent web is not emerging by default; the largest merchants are gating it, which means agent traffic will flow through negotiated channels rather than through the open web, and the firms that control those channels will set the terms. For an enterprise buyer, the question is no longer whether agents can buy, but which agents a given merchant will let through.

The payment layer moved in the same direction. Klarna and Adyen made Klarna the first payment option beyond cards inside Adyen's agentic suite on November 20, for background, and AWS Marketplace opened agent purchasing with spending limits and audit logging on November 19, for background.[^21] A holiday-shopping study cited in the same reporting found that a substantial share of agent users had an agent blocked from completing a purchase, and a smaller share said an agent bought something too expensive.

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration of agent identity as a governance problem. Spending limits, approved vendor lists and audit logs are becoming the standard controls for agent purchasing, which is exactly the governance stack the model predicted, and the failure modes — blocked purchases and overspending — are now measured rather than hypothetical. The next step to watch is whether merchants converge on a single verification standard for agent identity, or whether each channel keeps its own.

On the model supply chain, the retirement calendar is the week's most actionable item. Claude Sonnet 4.5 retires on November 30, and OpenAI's GPT-5 family retires in December, with named successors.[^15] Teams that pinned a model identifier will need to migrate, test and re-validate, and the cost of that migration is a real, if unglamorous, part of the total cost of an AI system.

Extrapolating forward, this is a level that continues an expected trend, but it tests the routing-economics claim. If routing is genuinely by cost and difficulty, a retirement is a configuration change; if a team has hard-coded a single model, it is a project. The discipline to build is a routing layer with a cost ledger and an evaluation harness, so that the next retirement is absorbed rather than escalated.

In healthcare, the week's enterprise story was already in motion before the window and continued into it. OpenAI's computer-use agents for healthcare, announced on November 16 for background, let agents navigate electronic health records and payer portals, with the company reporting that physicians rated nearly all reviewed clinical tasks as safe and that a smaller model handled administrative tasks for a few cents each.[^22] Those figures are the vendor's own evaluations, not independent trials, and they should be read as such.

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration of agent deployment into regulated, high-stakes workflows, and it tests the deployer-liability shift directly. An agent that clicks through a health record is acting inside a system of record, with audit logs and human review as the controls, and the firms that deploy it will need to show that those controls work in practice, not only in a vendor benchmark.

## Physical AI & Industrial Automation

Physical AI produced no new deployment numbers in the window that change the picture, and that absence is itself the finding. The best-documented deployments remain those reported before the window: humanoid robots doing narrow, repetitive work in warehouses and on a car production line, with operating hours and part counts attached, and with the robots still fragile and the work still narrow.[^23] No vendor in the window published a new production-scale deployment with comparable evidence.

Extrapolating forward, this is a level that continues an expected trend, and it supports the model's caution rather than the hype. Humanoids are moving from demonstration to paid work, but the work is a single task repeated many times, not general labour, and the capital question remains whether the cost and reliability thresholds for a second and third task can be met. A firm allocating capital here should ask for operating hours, intervention rates and cost per task, not for video.

The capital build-out around physical AI also stayed quiet in the window, in contrast to the data-centre build-out, which dominated the financing news. That contrast is worth stating plainly: data centres are being financed at a scale and speed that industrial automation has not yet matched, and the two build-outs are on different clocks. The data-centre clock is set by chip deliveries and power connections; the industrial clock is set by reliability, safety certification and integration into existing lines.

Extrapolating forward, this is a deceleration relative to the data-centre build-out, not a reversal of physical AI. The implication for a capital participant is to underwrite industrial automation on its own timetable, with milestones tied to demonstrated reliability, rather than importing the data-centre timetable and its financing structures wholesale.

On resilience, no publicly reported industrial cyber incident in the window changed the risk picture, and no new regulatory or insurance development for operational technology was found in the research window. That is a null result, and it should be recorded as such rather than filled with background. The standing risk — that a compromised component or a lost-containment agent could reach operational systems — remains as last week's edition described it, and the California lawsuit, filed on November 16 for background, remains the live legal test of that risk.[^24]

## Human and Machine Agency

This dimension gets a short treatment this week, because measured evidence on behaviour and institutions arrives monthly or quarterly, not weekly, and no scheduled study or dataset in the window changed the picture. Two documented events did move it, and both concern trust.

First, the privacy terms of consumer health AI changed in the open. OpenAI expanded ChatGPT Health to desktop and Android on November 19, for background, and at the same time changed its health privacy notice so that conversations drawing on connected health data can enter model training unless the user turns off a setting, reversing an earlier promise that such conversations would never be used for training.[^25] Reporting on the change in the window, on November 22, made the reversal visible to users who had not read the notice.

Extrapolating forward, this is a reversal, and it bears on trust as a leading indicator. A health product's value depends on users believing that sensitive data will not be reused, and a quiet change to that promise, even with an opt-out, teaches users to read the notice rather than the launch announcement. The firms that keep a clear, stable boundary around health data will find it easier to win the next deployment; the firms that move the boundary will find each move costs more trust than the last.

Second, agents moved onto the desktop, which changes the relationship between user and agent. An installed artifact app, as described in the orientation, can ask for notifications, sit in the menu bar, or request full access to the computer, and it updates automatically when the underlying artifact is edited, with each change prompting a new permission request.[^10] That is a new form of agency: software the user built by describing it, now living beside ordinary apps, with permissions the user must judge.

Extrapolating forward, this is a new trend in how people delegate. The user is no longer only chatting with an agent; the user is installing the agent's output as software, and the permission decision is the governance decision. The skill that matters is not prompt-writing but permission-judging, and organisations will need to teach it, because a single grant of full access can expose far more than a single chat ever could.

No new in-window evidence was found on AI companions, education, hiring or political movements that meets the bar for measured evidence, and none is invented here. The slow measures — public opinion on AI, entry-level hiring, AI use in schools and in health care, trust in information sources — are unchanged from last week and will be reported when a new reading arrives.

## Economic Structural Change

The week's economic story is the repricing, not a new statistic. No major official labour-market or productivity release in the window changed the aggregate picture, and the Federal Reserve's minutes, released on November 18 for background, remain the latest official word: officials judged that further rate rises would likely be appropriate, named AI demand as a material source of inflation risk, and noted that AI-related debt had grown rapidly through complex and opaque financing structures.[^26]

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration of the mechanism last week's edition described. The central bank is now treating the build-out as an inflation risk and a financial-stability risk at the same time, which means the cost of financing the build-out and the price of the goods it needs are linked through monetary policy. A higher rate path raises the cost of the very debt that funds the build-out, which is a self-limiting mechanism the market began to price in the window.

The market's verdict arrived quickly. The sell-off on November 24 and 25, described in the orientation, was broad: chipmakers led, large technology names fell, and volatility jumped, with the Nasdaq briefly flirting with a fall of a fifth from its record high.[^7][^8] The proximate triggers reported were renewed tariff proposals and stubborn inflation, but the underlying doubt was about returns: whether debt-funded capital spending will deliver the profit the market expects.

Extrapolating forward, this is a reversal in sentiment, not yet a reversal in spending. Capital expenditure plans, lease commitments and chip orders are set months ahead, so a week's trading does not cancel them, but it does raise the cost of the next financing and it tests the assumption that demand will absorb any supply. Watch the next financing pricing, not the next day's trading, for evidence of whether the repricing sticks.

On energy and power, the binding constraint did not move in the window, and that is the point. Loudoun County's pause on data-centre and substation decisions, voted on November 17 for background, remained in force, affecting dozens of pending applications while the county rewrites its standards.[^27] No new grid or interconnection decision in the window eased that constraint, and no new generation announcement matched its scale.

Extrapolating forward, this is a level that continues an expected trend, and it strengthens the shift from chips to permission as the constraint. Power and permission are still the scarce resources, and they are still local, political and slow, while chips and capital remain available, if more expensive. A project should be underwritten on whether it has secured power and permission, not only on whether it has secured a tenant.

On trade and industrial policy, the week's most concrete move was in quantum, covered in the quantum part, and in the Senate, where the AUDIT AI Act, introduced on November 19 for background, continued to be analysed in the window, with trade groups noting on November 24 that it would require disclosure of joint ventures, special-purpose vehicles and compute leases.[^28] That bill is a proposal, not law, and it will need to survive a new Congress, but it already changes the conversation: the question is no longer whether off-balance-sheet AI financing should be disclosed, but how.

## Institutional Financial Services

For institutional finance, the week tested three claims at once: that a frontier lab can be priced as a mature issuer, that AI infrastructure credit can be priced on the collateral, and that disclosure will catch up with financing.

Anthropic's first week as a public company failed the first test, at least for now. After opening well above its offer price and closing its first day higher still, the shares fell on every subsequent session until November 24, when they closed below the offer price for the first time, on the fourth day of trading.[^9] The fall was not a judgment on the company's technology, which continued to ship, but on its valuation: at the offer price the company was already valued at a level that assumed years of growth, and the market spent the week deciding how much of that growth to believe.

Extrapolating forward, this is a reversal in price discovery, not a reversal in the business. A broken IPO — one that falls below its offer price within days — is a familiar pattern for highly priced listings, and it does not by itself predict the company's future, but it does reset the reference price for every later AI listing and for every private valuation that was marked against it. Asset managers should treat the first week's trading as a repricing of expectations, not as a verdict on the model.

The financing structures that fund the build-out also faced their first market test in the window. The Apollo and Goldman Sachs financing for SpaceX, priced on November 20 for background at about forty-four billion dollars in notes to buy graphics processors, was oversubscribed and upsized, yet the shares of the borrower, the chipmaker and the arrangers all fell on the day it priced.[^29] That pairing — a successful financing and falling prices — is the anomaly the model predicted: scale and scrutiny rising together, with the market rewarding the completion of a deal while doubting the returns it will fund.

Extrapolating forward, this is an acceleration of the structured-credit shift. The financing was done through a special-purpose vehicle that buys the chips and leases them to the borrower, keeping the debt off the borrower's balance sheet, and the largest tranche carried a yield that compensates for the risk that the collateral depreciates faster than the lease pays down. Underwriting that credit requires diligence on residual value, utilisation and tenant concentration, not on the borrower's brand, and the firms that can do that diligence at scale will price it correctly.

Disclosure moved, but slowly. The AUDIT AI Act, introduced on November 19 for background, would direct the securities regulator to require disclosure of AI-related joint ventures, special-purpose vehicles, leases and guarantees, and of board oversight of AI risk.[^28] Analysis in the window stressed that the bill is only a proposal, that it would apply to arrangements made after a date the regulator chooses, and that older deals might escape, which means the transparency it promises would arrive gradually even if it passes.

Extrapolating forward, this is a new trend in the regulatory direction, but a deceleration in its timing. The direction is toward disclosure, and the market's repricing gives that direction political force, yet the legislative calendar and the effective-date rules mean that the obligations that matter today will remain partly hidden for some time. A credit team should not wait for the rule; it should already be asking borrowers for the lease and vehicle disclosures the bill would require.

On issuers' business models, the week's most telling evidence came from the market rather than from filings: the sell-off hit not only AI companies but the suppliers and customers around them, from chipmakers to cloud providers, which suggests the market is repricing the whole AI supply chain as a single trade rather than discriminating among its parts. That is a risk for a fixed-income manager, because a correlated fall in the trade can widen spreads across issuers that have little in common except their exposure to the build-out.

Extrapolating forward, this is a reversal in how the trade is priced, and it bears on duration and concentration. If AI-related issuance has grown as a share of new supply, and if its duration is long, then a repricing of the trade moves portfolios more than a repricing of a single name. The work to do is to measure that share and that duration in the portfolios the firm manages, rather than assuming diversification that the correlation has removed.

Agent-driven market flow produced no new in-window evidence of agentic trading accounts or agent-initiated payments at a scale that moves markets, and none is claimed here. The agent flow that did move was in commerce, covered in the enterprise part, where agents are now buying goods with spending limits and audit logs. That flow is a precursor: the plumbing for agent payments is being built in commerce first, and market flow will follow the same plumbing.

## Strategic Mental Model Shifts

### What changed in the model

From verification as a product to verification as a contested claim. The old version said verification is scarce and should be invested in as a first-class capability. This week showed a sharper fault line: a proof can be formally verified and still be contested, because the contest moves to the statement, the premises and the checker. The revised claim is that verification has layers — statement, derivation, certificate and understanding — and each layer can fail independently, so a certificate at one layer does not settle the others. That changes how the reader reasons across AI technology, quantum and finance: a checked result, a benchmark score and a credit rating are all layer-specific claims, and each needs its own audit.

From leadership as a durable asset to leadership as a reversible decision. The old model treated the binding constraint as permission and power, with leadership as a secondary factor. This week showed that a board can reverse a leadership decision in eleven days under government and market pressure, and that the reversal can move a company's value by about a third in a day. The revised claim is that governance at a systemically important AI supplier is now a market-moving variable in its own right, not a background condition, and that a board's independence, its ties to the founder and its exposure to government pressure are underwriting factors. That changes how the reader reasons about single-name risk in the build-out.

From price discovery as a verdict to price discovery as a whipsaw. The old model treated the Anthropic listing as evidence that the market would price a frontier lab as a mature issuer. This week showed the market pricing it as a mature issuer on day one and as an overpriced one by day four, while the Nasdaq swung by several per cent in a day on the same news. The revised claim is that price discovery for AI assets is now volatile enough to be a risk in itself, and that a week's trading can reverse a listing's signal without any change in the underlying business. That changes how the reader reasons about new issuance, private marks and index inclusion.

### What continued

From generation to verification held and strengthened, in the revised form above. From a single frontier to a portfolio of intelligences held: retirements, routing and the cost gap between frontier and smaller models all continued to reward a portfolio approach. From chips as the constraint to permission as the constraint held: Loudoun's pause remained in force and no new power decision eased it. From corporate credit to structured, collateral-specific credit held and strengthened: the SpaceX financing and its pricing showed the collateral, not the brand, setting the terms. From sandbox as engineering to sandbox as liability held and extended: desktop apps with full computer access brought the containment question onto the user's machine. From judgment as a by-product to judgment as the bottleneck held: the Riemann debate is, at its core, a judgment debate about what a proof is, and no machine settled it.

### Watching

- Whether the Navier-Stokes certificate is released and independently checked, and whether its verification follows the Riemann pattern or a different one.
- Whether the AUDIT AI Act gains a companion bill or regulator support, or stalls with the new Congress.
- Whether agent checkout converges on a single identity standard, or fragments by channel.
- Whether the AI sell-off changes the pricing of the next large GPU financing, or proves to be a week's trading.
- Whether a human-readable reconstruction of the Riemann proof appears, and how long understanding lags certification.
